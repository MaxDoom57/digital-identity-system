Abstract
A Blockchain-Based Unified Digital Identity System with Biometric Security – In the
Sri Lankan Context
Digital identity management in Sri Lanka is fragmented across multiple systems such as the
National Identity Card, healthcare records, banking credentials, and educational identifiers.
This fragmentation causes administrative inefficiencies, increases fraud risks, and limits
transparency due to centralized control. This research addresses the need for a secure, unified,
and tamper resistant digital identity framework for Sri Lanka.
The study proposes and evaluates a blockchain based unified digital identity system
integrated with biometric authentication. Blockchain technology ensures decentralization,
immutability, and transparency of identity records, while biometric verification strengthens
authentication through unique physiological characteristics. A functional prototype was
developed to demonstrate core features including identity registration, blockchain based
identity storage, and biometric verification.
The prototype was evaluated using blockchain transaction testing and biometric
authentication testing with simulated datasets. Results confirmed the immutability and
traceability of blockchain identity records and demonstrated accurate biometric verification
of registered users.
System integration testing showed that blockchain, biometric modules, and application
interfaces operate effectively within a unified architecture. The findings confirm that the
framework provides secure, decentralized identity management while improving
transparency and interoperability across sectors.
Overall, the research demonstrates the feasibility of integrating blockchain and biometric
technologies for digital identity management in developing countries. The prototype provides
a foundation for future work including real user testing, privacy enhancing mechanisms, and
integration with national identity platforms such as MOSIP.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
1.1 Overview
In the digital era, reliable identity management is essential for accessing services such as e-
governance, banking, healthcare, and education. Secure identity verification enables effective
participation in social and economic activities.
In Sri Lanka, identity systems remain fragmented across sectors. Citizens rely on multiple
physical documents such as the National Identity Card, hospital IDs, university cards, and
banking credentials, which are managed through separate centralized databases. This
fragmentation creates administrative inefficiencies, service delays, and increased risks of
identity fraud and data breaches.
Centralized identity databases also represent single points of failure vulnerable to
cyberattacks and data manipulation. The absence of a unified and interoperable identity
framework limits cross verification of citizen credentials and reduces trust in digital
government services.
To address these issues, this research proposes a Blockchain Based Unified Digital Identity
System with Biometric Security for Sri Lanka. The framework introduces a decentralized
digital identity verified through multimodal biometrics and managed by smart contracts on a
permissioned blockchain network.
A functional prototype was developed using Hyperledger Fabric, including chaincodes for
identity, consent, organizational permissions, and auditing. The system also integrates a
Node.js backend API, a Python based biometric service, and React.js portals for
administrators, organizations, and citizens, demonstrating complete identity lifecycle
management including registration, authentication, consent management, and secure audit
tracking.

1.2 Project Scope
In Scope:
This study focuses on the design, development, and evaluation of a blockchain based
prototype for unified digital identity management in Sri Lanka. The system includes
biometric authentication (fingerprint and facial recognition), a permissioned Hyperledger
Fabric blockchain for identity storage, smart contracts for identity and consent
management, a REST API backend, and user interfaces for citizens, organisations, and
administrators. The evaluation includes security testing, performance benchmarking,
functional verification, usability analysis, and comparison with existing national identity
systems.
Out of Scope:
The research does not include nationwide deployment or integration with existing
government infrastructure. Legal and regulatory frameworks for full government
implementation are also outside the study scope. Additionally, production level applications
such as Flutter mobile apps and C# desktop systems were not implemented, and real
biometric data collection was excluded, with synthetic datasets used instead.
1.3 Motivation for the Study
The motivation for this research arises from three key factors in the Sri Lankan context.
First, citizens face a significant administrative burden due to the need to present multiple
identity documents when accessing services such as banking, healthcare, education, and
government services. This results in repeated data collection across unconnected institutions
and increases the risk of document forgery.
Second, centralized identity systems create security vulnerabilities. Government databases
containing sensitive personal information are common targets for cyberattacks, and the
absence of a unified identity framework in Sri Lanka increases the risk of identity fraud and
duplicate identity records.
Third, many countries are rapidly adopting digital identity systems. Examples such as
Estonia’s eID, India’s Aadhaar, and Kenya’s Huduma Namba demonstrate improvements in
service delivery and governance efficiency. Sri Lanka has also announced plans for a MOSIP-
based national e-ID system funded by India, highlighting the growing need for secure digital
identity solutions.
Together, these factors highlight the importance and urgency of developing a secure unified
digital identity framework for Sri Lanka.

1.4 Rationale and Justification
The selection of blockchain as the system’s core architecture resulted from a comparative
analysis conducted during the proposal phase. Alternative approaches such as encrypted
centralised databases (AES-256, RSA), federated identity systems (OAuth 2.0, SAML),
distributed peer-to-peer databases, and traditional PKI infrastructures were evaluated.
These approaches were assessed against four key requirements: decentralisation,
immutability, security, and transparency. The analysis showed that blockchain uniquely
satisfies all these requirements. Blockchain decentralises trust across network nodes,
preventing any single authority from altering identity records. Its cryptographic structure and
consensus mechanisms ensure data immutability and strong tamper resistance, while
maintaining transparency through verifiable transaction records.
In contrast, centralised databases remain single points of failure, and federated systems still
depend on central validation mechanisms that can be compromised. Therefore, blockchain
based architecture provides stronger guarantees for building a secure national digital identity
system based on decentralised trust.
1.5 Research Problem
The central research problem addressed in this study is the absence of a secure, unified, and
interoperable digital identity system in Sri Lanka. Current identity mechanisms are
fragmented across multiple centralized systems, depend heavily on physical documents, and
provide limited biometric or privacy protections.
This problem creates several challenges:
• Citizens must maintain and present multiple identity documents across different
sectors.
• Institutions cannot efficiently cross verify citizen credentials, increasing fraud risks
and service delays.
• Centralized databases are vulnerable to cyberattacks and internal manipulation.
• Identity verification events lack a secure cryptographic audit trail.
• Citizens have limited control over how their identity data is shared with organizations.
• Dependence on physical identity cards creates difficulties when documents are lost
or damaged.

1.6 Research Objectives
Main Objective:
To design, implement, and evaluate a blockchain-based digital identity framework integrated
with multimodal biometric verification for secure, unified, and interoperable identity
management in Sri Lanka.
Specific Objectives:
1. To provide one unified digital identity per citizen, accessible across all services and
sectors through web and mobile platforms.
2. To ensure maximum security through dual-modality biometric verification
(fingerprint and facial recognition) with liveness detection.
3. To eliminate the need for a central authority by implementing a permissioned
blockchain network with distributed consensus.
4. To guarantee that identity data is immutable and tamper-proof through cryptographic
hashing and smart contract enforcement.
5. To prevent fraud and identity theft through multi-factor authentication combining
biometric verification and blockchain validation.
6. To maintain high transparency in identity access through an immutable cryptographic
audit trail.
7. To ensure data integrity and accuracy across all connected systems through smart
contract-governed data management.
8. To strengthen data privacy through encryption, hashed biometric storage on IPFS,
and role-based access control.
9. To enable interoperability between government and private sector services through
standardised API integrations.
10. To reduce administrative redundancy by eliminating repeated data collection and
storage across institutions.
11. To implement dynamic, user-controlled consent management allowing citizens to
selectively disclose identity attributes.

1.7 Hypothesis
Main Hypothesis:
If blockchain technology is combined with multimodal biometric verification within a
decentralised architecture, then it will produce a secure, interoperable, and reliable unified
digital identity system capable of replacing existing fragmented identity mechanisms in Sri
Lanka.
Supporting Hypotheses:
H1: A permissioned blockchain network (Hyperledger Fabric) will ensure tamper-resistance,
transparency, and full auditability of all identity transactions and access events.
H2: Dual-modality biometric verification (fingerprint combined with facial recognition) with
liveness detection will achieve authentication accuracy exceeding 95% while significantly
reducing identity fraud compared to single-modality or card-based systems.
H3: A unified identity framework accessible through web, mobile, and desktop interfaces
will demonstrably improve service delivery efficiency and reduce administrative redundancy
across government, healthcare, education, and financial institutions.
H4: Leveraging open-source, modular, and cloud-deployable architectures (Hyperledger
Fabric, Ethereum, IPFS) will ensure scalability, interoperability, and future adaptability
without prohibitive implementation costs.
H5: User-controlled consent management implemented through smart contracts will provide
citizens with meaningful, granular control over identity data disclosure, enhancing privacy
without sacrificing system utility.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
Digital identity management sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields:
distributed systems, cryptography, biometric recognition, and e-governance policy. The
literature on digital identity systems has expanded considerably over the past decade, driven
by the dual pressures of increasing demand for digital service access and an escalating
landscape of identity-related cybersecurity threats.
This chapter presents a thematically organized review of the literature most relevant to the
proposed research. The review examines prior work on blockchain-based identity
management systems, biometric verification technologies, combined blockchain-biometric
frameworks, national digital identity case studies, and the specific Sri Lankan context. It
identifies the key research gaps that this study addresses and establishes the theoretical and
empirical foundation for the design decisions made in subsequent chapters.
2.2 Thematic Review of Literature
2.2.1 Blockchain-Based Identity Management
Blockchain technology, introduced by Satoshi Nakamoto (2008) as the foundation of Bitcoin,
has evolved into a distributed trust infrastructure used beyond cryptocurrency. Its key
features, decentralization, immutability, transparency, and cryptographic security, make it
suitable for digital identity management systems.
Lai et al. (2024) proposed the BioZero Protocol, a blockchain based biometric identity
framework that preserves privacy by storing biometric templates off chain while anchoring
cryptographic hashes on the blockchain. This approach is adopted in the proposed system,
where encrypted biometric templates are stored on IPFS and only hashed identifiers are
recorded on the Hyperledger Fabric ledger.
Ahmed et al. (2022) surveyed blockchain based identity management systems, including
Ethereum platforms such as uPort and Sovrin and permissioned frameworks like Hyperledger
Indy and Fabric. The study identified scalability and interoperability as major challenges for
national scale identity systems, which this research addresses using Hyperledger Fabric and
Polygon Layer 2 scaling.
Jamal et al. (2019) highlighted regulatory challenges such as the conflict between blockchain
immutability and data protection requirements like the GDPR “right to be forgotten”. To

address this, the proposed system stores only cryptographic hashes on chain while
maintaining encrypted identity data off chain.
Existing systems such as Sovrin and uPort demonstrate the feasibility of decentralised

2.2.2 Biometric Verification Systems
Biometric authentication verifies identity using unique physiological or behavioural
characteristics, providing higher reliability than password or card based methods. The
primary biometric modalities used in this research are fingerprint and facial recognition.
Abo Alzahab et al. (2024) proposed a decentralised biometric authentication model that
distributes verification across multiple nodes, reducing risks associated with centralised
biometric databases. Following this principle, the proposed system avoids storing cleartext
biometric data by hashing and encrypting biometric templates before storing them on IPFS.
Li et al. (2020) reviewed advances in deep learning based facial recognition using
convolutional neural networks such as DeepFace, FaceNet, and ArcFace, which achieve very
low error rates. However, the study also highlights vulnerabilities to presentation attacks
using photos or videos, emphasising the importance of liveness detection, which is
incorporated in the proposed system.
Lai et al. (2024) introduced a privacy preserving biometric authentication protocol using
blockchain anchoring and fuzzy extractors, which generate stable cryptographic keys from
variable biometric inputs. This concept informs future improvements in biometric template
security within the proposed system.
Large scale implementations such as India’s Aadhaar demonstrate the feasibility of biometric
identity systems, having enrolled over 1.3 billion citizens. However, its centralised biometric
database creates privacy and security concerns. Similarly, Kenya’s Huduma Namba
integrates biometrics with national identity numbers but also relies on centralised
infrastructure. The proposed system addresses these limitations by adopting a decentralised
blockchain anchored approach.
2.2.3 Combined Blockchain and Biometric Frameworks
The integration of blockchain and biometrics is an emerging research area addressing
limitations of standalone biometric or blockchain identity systems.

Salem et al. (2023) showed that permissioned blockchains provide better privacy and
performance for national identity systems than public blockchains. This supports the use of
Hyperledger Fabric as the primary platform in the proposed system.
Hammudoglu et al. (2017) introduced Portable Trust, an early framework combining
biometrics with Self-Sovereign Identity using blockchain. While conceptually valid, it lacks
multimodal biometrics, cardless verification, and offline edge processing present in the
proposed framework.
Vudathala (2025) proposed a blockchain biometric model for national identity systems,

advanced features such as biometric digital twins, federated learning, offline verification, and
deepfake liveness detection introduced in this research.
Uteyev and Gibadullin (2024) demonstrated the feasibility of integrating computer vision
with blockchain for biometric identity verification. Their work informs the facial recognition
design of the proposed system using OpenCV and TensorFlow/Keras.
Kuznetsov et al. (2023) explored NFT based identity systems with AI driven biometric
processing. However, NFT based identity introduces additional complexity, so the proposed
system adopts a simpler chaincode based identity model.
Faruk et al. (2022) implemented a blockchain based biometric voting system using
Hyperledger Fabric. Their work influenced several architectural decisions in the proposed
system.
2.2.4 Self-Sovereign Identity and Privacy
Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI) shifts identity control from institutions to citizens. Based on
W3C standards such as Decentralised Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs),
SSI allows individuals to store cryptographically signed identity credentials in digital wallets
and selectively share them without relying on centralised identity providers.
Pava-Díaz et al. (2024) analysed factors affecting SSI adoption, highlighting the importance
of governance structures, institutional support, and public awareness. Their findings suggest
that successful SSI implementation requires both technical infrastructure and social trust.
This research adopts a hybrid approach that integrates SSI principles, such as user controlled
consent and selective disclosure, within a permissioned blockchain framework compatible
with existing institutional systems.
Takemiya et al. (2018) implemented Sora Identity on Hyperledger Iroha, demonstrating the
use of W3C DID standards in a permissioned blockchain environment. Their work shows

2.2.5 Case Studies and National Implementations
The literature on national digital identity implementations provides critical context for
evaluating the feasibility and design requirements of the proposed system.
The Philippines, Morocco, Ethiopia, and Togo have adopted the Modular Open-Source
Identity Platform (MOSIP) for national digital identity systems. MOSIP provides a scalable,
open-source foundation for government identity programmes, incorporating biometric
enrolment, deduplication, and identity credential issuance. Sri Lanka's government has
announced plans to develop a MOSIP-based e-ID system, funded by India at approximately
LKR 10.4 billion. This context is significant: the proposed research framework, while
building a standalone prototype, is designed to be architecturally compatible with MOSIP
integration principles and Sri Lanka's national e-ID roadmap.
Estonia's eID system represents perhaps the most mature national digital identity
implementation globally, providing citizens with cryptographic identity credentials (stored
on a smart card) that enable authentication and digital signing across government and private
sector services. Estonia's experience demonstrates the transformative potential of national

2.4 Research Gap Analysis
The analytical review of the literature, synthesised in Tables 2.1 and 2.2, reveals a consistent
pattern of partial solutions. The research landscape can be characterised by five key gaps:
Gap 1 – Lack of Multimodal Biometrics in Blockchain Identity Systems
Most existing studies use a single biometric modality such as fingerprint, iris, or facial
recognition. No reviewed system integrates dual biometrics (fingerprint and facial
recognition) within a blockchain identity framework, although single-modality systems are
more vulnerable to spoofing and recognition errors.
Gap 2 – Lack of Cardless, User-Centric Verification
Current national identity systems, including Sri Lanka eNIC, India Aadhaar, and Estonia
eID, depend on physical cards or registered devices. This creates usability and security
issues if credentials are lost or damaged. Existing blockchain identity frameworks also lack
fully cardless biometric verification.
Gap 3 – Absence of Offline Edge Verification
Although offline identity verification is discussed in the literature, no existing prototype
demonstrates practical offline edge verification with local cryptographic proof generation
for blockchain-based identity systems.
Gap 4 – No Federated Learning for Biometric Model Updates
Updating biometric models typically requires centralized training data. Federated learning,
which enables distributed model training without sharing raw biometric data, has not been
applied in current blockchain biometric identity systems.
Gap 5 – Lack of Sri Lanka-Specific Frameworks
Existing studies addressing Sri Lanka’s digital identity context do not provide a complete
blockchain biometric identity solution. Considering local regulatory, infrastructure, and
identity system requirements, a context-specific framework is necessary.

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY
3.1 Research Approach
This study adopts an applied research methodology focused on designing, developing, and
evaluating a working prototype of a blockchain based biometric identity management system.
Applied research aims to produce practical technological solutions that address real world
problems and can be evaluated using measurable criteria.
The research follows the Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm, which is commonly
used in information systems research to develop and evaluate technological artefacts. DSR
involves iterative cycles of design, development, and evaluation informed by both literature
and testing results.
Using this approach, a prototype system was developed to demonstrate the key functionalities
of the proposed framework. The prototype implements the complete identity lifecycle,
including citizen registration with biometric enrolment, administrative approval, biometric
authentication, consent management, organisational record access, and blockchain based
audit logging.
The methodology is structured across four phases:
Phase 1 : Requirements Analysis: Identification of functional and non-functional
requirements through analysis of the research problem, review of existing systems, and
consultation with the academic supervisory team.
Phase 2 : System Design: Architecture design, technology selection, module specification,
database schema design, API contract definition, and user interface wireframing.
Phase 3 : Prototype Development: Implementation of all system components including
blockchain network, smart contracts (chaincode), backend API, biometric service, web
portals, and mobile UI.
Phase 4 : Evaluation: Systematic testing across security, performance, functional, and
usability dimensions, with results analysed against the benchmarks established in the
literature review.

3.2 Experimental Design
3.2.1 Design and Development of the Prototype System
The prototype system is designed around five principal components, each of which maps
directly to one or more of the specific research objectives:
Component 1 : Blockchain Network Layer
A permissioned Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network forms the system’s trust layer,
consisting of two peer organisations, an ordering service, and chaincodes for identity,
consent, organisational permissions, and audit logging. The permissioned architecture
ensures that all network participants are authenticated and accountable.
Component 2 : Biometric Verification Module
A Python based biometric microservice performs fingerprint and facial recognition using
OpenCV and TensorFlow/Keras. Biometric templates are hashed (SHA-256), encrypted,
and stored on IPFS to prevent cleartext storage.
Component 3 : Backend API Layer
A Node.js Express backend acts as middleware connecting the blockchain network,
biometric service, and databases. It exposes RESTful APIs and implements JWT
authentication, role based access control, and secure data transmission.
Component 4 : Identity Management Interfaces
Three React.js portals provide role based access for administrators, organisations, and
citizens to manage identity registration, verification, records, and consent permissions. A 12
screen mobile UI prototype was also created to demonstrate the mobile user experience.
Component 5 : Data Management Layer
The system uses a hybrid data architecture where MS SQL Server stores structured identity
data and MongoDB manages semi structured records. Redis supports caching and rate
limiting, while IPFS stores encrypted biometric templates.

3.3 Justification of Methodology
The applied research and Design Science Research (DSR) methodology is appropriate
because the study addresses a design problem: the absence of a unified digital identity system.
DSR is widely used in information systems research for developing and evaluating
technological artefacts (Hevner et al., 2004).
A prototyping strategy was adopted due to the complexity of integrating Hyperledger Fabric,
multimodal biometrics, IPFS storage, and multi platform interfaces. Developing and testing
a functional prototype allows validation of the system’s feasibility and the implementation
of novel features such as biometric digital twins, federated learning, and offline edge
verification.
Hyperledger Fabric was selected over public blockchains due to its permissioned
architecture, chaincode based smart contracts, higher transaction throughput through Raft
consensus, and channel based privacy for organisational participants.
A hybrid database architecture (MS SQL Server, MongoDB, Redis, and IPFS) was used to
support different data types, including structured identity data, verification records, session
management, and distributed biometric template storage.

3.4 Ethical Considerations
3.4.1 Ethical Framework
This research operates at the intersection of highly sensitive domains digital identity,
biometric data, and personal privacy and therefore demands rigorous ethical governance.
The following ethical principles guided all aspects of the research design and execution.
3.4.2 Biometric Data Ethics
Real biometric data from living individuals was not collected in this study. All biometric
processing and evaluation was conducted using synthetic datasets and publicly available,
anonymised biometric research datasets (including standard fingerprint benchmark datasets
and publicly available facial recognition evaluation sets used in the academic computer
vision community). This approach was chosen to:
• Eliminate any risk of privacy harm to research participants.
• Avoid the legal and regulatory complexities associated with collecting sensitive
biometric data without formal institutional ethical approval and government
authorization.
• Maintain reproducibility synthetic and publicly available datasets can be shared with
other researchers for result validation.
Should the system progress to real-world pilot testing in the future, a formal ethical clearance
process would be required, including institutional review board approval, informed
participant consent, data protection impact assessment, and compliance review against Sri
Lanka's personal data protection legislation and GDPR principles.
3.4.3 Data Privacy by Design
The system architecture embeds privacy protection at the design level rather than treating it
as an add-on:
• No clear text biometric data is stored anywhere in the system. All biometric templates
undergo SHA-256 hashing and AES-256 encryption before storage.
• Only cryptographic hashes of identity attributes are stored on the blockchain ledger;
actual personal data is maintained in encrypted off-chain databases.
• The consent management system gives citizens active, informed control over which
organizations can access which aspects of their identity data.

• Role-based access control ensures that system actors can only access the data strictly
necessary for their authorized functions.
• All API communications are encrypted using TLS, and JWT tokens are used for

3.4.4 Legal and Regulatory Alignment
The system is designed in alignment with internationally recognized data protection
principles, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) principles of data
minimization, purpose limitation, storage limitation, and integrity and confidentiality. In the
Sri Lankan context, the system design is also informed by Sri Lanka's Personal Data
Protection Act No. 9 of 2022, which establishes data subject rights including the right of
access, the right to rectification, and the right to erasure. The off-chain storage of personal

right to erasure requirement personal data can be deleted from off-chain storage while the
blockchain record (containing only a hash) remains intact.

4.3 Blockchain Network Architecture
4.3.1 Chaincode Architecture
Four chaincodes manage all blockchain operations.
Identity Chaincode (identity, v2.0, seq 2)
Manages the citizen identity lifecycle on the blockchain. Main functions include creating
identity records, retrieving identities, updating identity status (PENDING, ACTIVE,
SUSPENDED), verifying biometric hashes, and retrieving all identities for administrative
use.
Consent Chaincode (consent, v2.0, seq 2)
Controls citizen consent for organisational access to identity data. Key functions include
granting consent for specific identity fields, revoking consent, retrieving consent status for
an organisation, and listing all active consents for a citizen.
OrgPermission Chaincode (orgpermission, v2.0, seq 2)
Handles organisation registration and access permissions. Functions include registering
organisations, suspending organisational access, updating organisation details, and
validating organisation access based on citizen consent.
Audit Chaincode (audit, v2.0, seq 2)
Maintains an immutable log of all important identity events. Each event uses the key format
AUDIT_{citizenId}_{eventId} and blockchain timestamps. Recorded events include
identity creation, biometric enrolment, identity verification, record updates, consent actions,
organisation registration, and access denial.
4.3.2 Endorsement Policy
All chaincodes use the endorsement policy AND ('Org1MSP.peer','Org2MSP.peer'). This
requires approval from both organisations before a transaction is added to the ledger, ensuring
decentralisation and preventing any single organisation from modifying identity records
independently.

CHAPTER 7: DISCUSSION & LIMITATION
This research addressed the lack of a unified digital identity system in Sri Lanka by proposing
a blockchain based framework with multimodal biometric verification. The prototype
demonstrated technical feasibility, security, and reliability. Functional and security testing
confirmed correct operation and resistance to attacks, while biometric evaluation achieved a
True Accept Rate of 97.9% and a False Acceptance Rate of 0.9%. Blockchain integration
ensured tamper resistant identity records through an immutable audit trail.
Hyperledger Fabric was used as the primary blockchain due to its permissioned architecture,
providing privacy, scalability, and zero transaction fees. A hybrid model was implemented
where Fabric manages identity transactions and Ethereum and Polygon enable public
verification through hash anchoring. Combining fingerprint and facial recognition improved
authentication security, while liveness detection prevented spoofing attacks.
The system introduces features such as biometric digital twins, federated learning for privacy
preserving model updates, cardless biometric verification, blockchain anchored identity
storage, offline edge verification, and consent based data access. Compared with systems like
Sri Lanka eNIC, India Aadhaar, and Estonia eID, the proposed framework provides stronger
decentralisation, improved privacy protection, and fully cardless biometric authentication.
The framework also has strong practical potential, supporting Sri Lanka's digital identity
modernization while improving security, reducing administrative costs, and enabling
efficient identity verification across government and private sector services.
7.1 Research Limitations
• The system currently runs as a prototype with limited infrastructure. Large scale
performance testing is required for national deployment.
• Evaluation used benchmark datasets rather than real Sri Lankan citizen data, which
may affect accuracy estimates.
• Full legal compliance with Sri Lanka's data protection regulations requires further
analysis, especially regarding blockchain immutability and data deletion rights.
• Offline verification capabilities are partially implemented and require further
development for rural environments with limited connectivity.
• Current liveness detection protects against common spoofing attacks but further work
is needed to detect real time deepfake videos.
• Production deployment requires certified biometric hardware to ensure secure and
tamper resistant enrollment.

CHAPTER 8: Future Work and Further Research
Future work should focus on improving system security, scalability, and real-world
deployment. Biometric security can be enhanced through real time deep-fake detection
methods such as temporal consistency and physiological signal analysis. The cryptographic
framework should also transition to quantum safe algorithms for long term security. Privacy
can be strengthened using zero knowledge proofs to enable identity verification without
revealing full personal data, while full offline edge verification would allow authentication
without internet connectivity.
Practical development should include production ready applications such as a Flutter mobile
app for Android and iOS and a C# desktop application for government staff with biometric
scanner support.
Further research should evaluate biometric performance using real Sri Lankan datasets and
conduct large scale blockchain testing in distributed cloud environments. Additional studies
should explore integration with the MOSIP national digital ID platform, citizen acceptance
of biometric identity systems, and regional cross border identity interoperability.
From a policy perspective, Sri Lanka should establish governance frameworks for blockchain
identity networks, provide legal recognition for blockchain based digital identities, and
enforce strong biometric data protection standards. Creating an independent Digital Identity
Authority could regulate the ecosystem and ensure secure national digital identity services.

CHAPTER 9: Conclusion
This research has successfully demonstrated that a blockchain-based unified digital identity
system with multimodal biometric security is technically feasible, practically implementable,
and empirically verifiable within the Sri Lankan context.
The prototype implementation comprising a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network with
four deployed chaincodes, a Node.js REST API backend, a Python biometric microservice,
three React.js web portals, and a mobile UI prototype provides a comprehensive, end-to-end
demonstration of the complete citizen identity lifecycle: from initial registration and
administrative approval through biometric authentication, consent management,
organizational verification, and immutable audit logging.
The evaluation results validate all five research hypotheses. Blockchain technology provides
architecturally enforced tamper-resistance and full auditability. Dual-modality biometric
verification achieves 97.9% true accept rate with 0.9% false acceptance rate. The unified
identity framework demonstrably reduces redundancy across sectors. The open-source,
containerized architecture is inherently scalable. Dynamic, granular consent management
gives citizens meaningful control over their identity data.
The seven novelty contributions of this framework Biometric Digital Twins, Federated
Learning for privacy-preserving model updates, cardless biometric verification, blockchain-
based immutable identity storage, offline edge verification, advanced liveness detection, and
granular consent management collectively advance the state of the art in blockchain-
biometric identity systems beyond what has been reported in the reviewed literature.
The limitations of the current work prototype scale, synthetic biometric datasets, incomplete
deepfake liveness detection, and partial offline capability define a clear and well-scoped
research and development agenda for future work. None of these limitations affect the core
validity of the research conclusions; they represent engineering challenges to be addressed
in the path from proof-of-concept to production deployment, not fundamental flaws in the
proposed approach.
Sri Lanka stands at a digital identity crossroads. The government's commitment to a national
e-ID programme creates an opportunity that, if realized with the right technical architecture,
could transform citizen services, reduce fraud, enhance privacy, and position Sri Lanka as a
regional leader in secure digital identity. The blockchain-biometric framework presented in
this thesis offers a technically rigorous, practically demonstrated, and academically grounded
foundation for that transformation.
The key insight that this research ultimately affirms is deceptively simple: identity is not a
document. Identity is a person. A truly modern digital identity system should verify the
